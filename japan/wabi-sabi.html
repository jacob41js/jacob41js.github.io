Personal Reflections

This is more of a handbook than an argument. The title should be taken literally. What this book does is provide a concrete framework for wabi-sabi aesthetics, judgement, and ethics rather than argue about any of those points. If Koren is right, then the shadows and darkness idealized in In Praise of Shadows resonate in wabi-sabi as well. Beautiful things have their basis in the natural, imperfect order. Shadows haunting the crevices of non-symmetrical forms are welcome. But there’s also the way light plays with unfinished, damaged, or earthy surfaces. Wabi-sabi does not place value on the high-sheen smoothness of modernity. It is unprepossessing in its hiddenness, almost camouflage, waiting for the appreciating eye to find it. And it esteems the imprecision of human hands, something I can relate with every time I super glue another Pokémon figurine or stitch an American Girl outfit.

I’m reminded of Nietzsche’s slave morality and wonder if it has a corollary in aesthetics. If Christianity suppresses the supposedly rightful will of the individual, does wabi-sabi or the tea ceremony do something similar with aesthetics? Of course, Nietzsche viewed this as arising from resentment, but Christianity, in its purest form, asks us to simplify and appreciate the work of God everywhere we look, rather than trying to shape this world in our image. Perhaps the analogy is strained, but wabi-sabi seems to be asking for something similar. It is not grasping outward, trying to twist the world into its desired shape, but accepting the world as it is, looking inward to see how we fit into the whole.

The simplicity of the tea room — modeled after a simple farmer’s hut — contains layers of meaning without artificiality. In it you have the natural materials used to construct it, the way the translucence of light modifies the interior, the simple décor meant to evoke scenes from nature, and the years of use that inevitably show despite the care taken to keep it in good order. Time, light, space, the most basic materials in the universe, coalesce into something subtle and transcendent.

Koren makes it clear that there is no official “school” of wabi-sabi. No doctrine that governs the movement. It has been an organic thing for centuries, so formalizing it in this book is a stretch. However, I think it does a passable job at providing an articulation of the essence of wabi-sabi, even if you must remember that it is only an articulation and not the whole thing. Spiritually it seems to jive with In Praise of Shadows and The Book of Tea, even if it is speaking from the outside, because it very clearly draws a contrast between the particular Japanese aesthetic it’s describing and modernity. Which makes it all very interesting when you consider what the common notion of Japanese culture is today. Either these movements have become passé like Victorian design, or they have been somehow sublimated into contemporary culture in a way I can’t quite see yet. I suspect, eventually, I’ll come to understand that evolution a bit better.
